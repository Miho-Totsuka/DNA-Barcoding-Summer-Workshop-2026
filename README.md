# DNA Barcoding Summer Workshop 2026

## Overview

I participated in a five-day DNA science workshop at the DNA Learning Center (DNALC). During the workshop, I learned how DNA can be extracted, amplified, separated, sequenced, and used to identify organisms.

The workshop combined hands-on laboratory experiments with concepts in molecular biology and DNA analysis. I worked with different biological and environmental samples and learned how techniques such as PCR, gel electrophoresis, DNA barcoding, metabarcoding, Sanger sequencing, and Nanopore sequencing can be used to study DNA.

## Five-Day Journey

### Day 1 — DNA Extraction and Basic Laboratory Techniques

I learned how to use micropipettes and practiced working with very small liquid volumes. I also learned the basic process of DNA extraction.

DNA extraction involved breaking open cells to release DNA, allowing the DNA to bind to a purification material, washing away unwanted substances, and finally eluting the purified DNA.

I worked with a plant sample and learned how DNA can later be analyzed using gel electrophoresis and DNA barcoding.

### Day 2 — DNA Extraction and Sanger Sequencing

I continued practicing DNA extraction using biological samples and learned more about silica-based DNA purification.

I used centrifugation, washing, and elution steps to separate DNA from other cellular material.

I also learned how Sanger sequencing works. One important concept was the difference between dNTPs and ddNTPs. DNA polymerase normally adds dNTPs while copying DNA, but when a ddNTP is incorporated, DNA synthesis stops. By producing DNA fragments of different lengths, the sequence of the DNA can be determined.

[Day 2 README](DAY2/README.md)

### Day 3 — Soil DNA, PCR, and Metabarcoding

I worked with a soil sample and learned how DNA can be extracted from an environmental sample containing DNA from many different organisms.

I also practiced calculating concentrations and volumes using:

`C₁V₁ = C₂V₂`

I learned how PCR is used to amplify selected regions of DNA using primers, DNA polymerase, nucleotides, and repeated cycles of heating and cooling.

I was introduced to DNA metabarcoding, which makes it possible to analyze DNA from many organisms in a mixed environmental sample instead of identifying only one organism at a time.

[Day 3 README](DAY3/README.md)

### Day 4 — DNA Sequencing and Identification

I examined DNA produced during the previous experiments and learned more about DNA sequencing.

One method I learned about was Nanopore sequencing. In this method, DNA passes through a very small pore. Different nucleotides cause different changes in electrical current, and a computer analyzes these signals to determine the DNA sequence.

I also learned how DNA sequence information can help identify organisms and how this can be compared with classification based only on morphology.

## DNA Barcoding and Metabarcoding

One of the most interesting ideas I learned was the difference between DNA barcoding and DNA metabarcoding.

DNA barcoding uses a selected DNA region to help identify an individual organism.

DNA metabarcoding applies a similar idea to mixed samples containing DNA from many organisms. Instead of analyzing one organism at a time, many DNA sequences can be analyzed together to investigate which organisms may be present in an environmental sample.

This was especially interesting to me because it connects laboratory biology with DNA sequence analysis.

## From Sample to Sequence

One of the most useful things I learned during the workshop was how the different techniques fit together.

A simplified workflow is:

`Sample → DNA Extraction → DNA Purification → PCR → Gel Electrophoresis → DNA Sequencing → Sequence Analysis → Organism Identification`

Before this workshop, I knew about several of these methods separately. Performing and learning about them as part of one workflow helped me understand how biological samples can eventually become DNA sequence data that can be analyzed on a computer.

## Connection to My Interests

This helped me understand that bioinformatics is closely connected to experimental biology: the quality of the final data depends on how samples are collected, DNA is extracted, PCR is performed, and sequences are generated.

## Reflection

The most interesting part of the workshop was seeing the connection between physical biological samples and digital DNA sequence data.

A small sample of plant material, an organism, or soil can eventually be converted into DNA sequences that can be compared and analyzed. I also learned that every stage of the process can affect the final result, from pipetting and DNA extraction to PCR and sequencing.

The workshop made me more interested in understanding not only how DNA sequences are analyzed computationally, but also how those sequences are produced in the laboratory.
